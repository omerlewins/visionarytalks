## The earlier businesses are separate transactions

The [Forbes profile](https://www.forbes.com/profile/brett-adcock/) identifies Adcock’s University of Florida education, Vettery and Archer before Figure. It says Vettery was sold to Adecco in 2018 for $110 million. The reported $110 million concerns the acquisition, rather than his personal proceeds. We attribute the reported price and do not turn it into personal proceeds.

A founder can hold less than all of a company at exit. Taxes, cofounders, investors and securities with different rights can change the amount attributable to an individual. The company sale price therefore cannot be rolled directly into a personal wealth ledger. A useful reconstruction would need the pre-sale ownership and transaction terms.

The sequence also crosses business models. A hiring marketplace, electric aviation and humanoid robotics have different operating constraints. An earlier exit can provide experience or resources without proving that a later venture has the same capital needs or path to revenue.

## Figure creates a physical deployment question

Forbes dates Figure’s founding to 2022 and describes humanoid robots. Our analysis is that the financial question extends beyond a demonstration of physical capability. A customer needs dependable operation, maintenance and integration with a real worksite. The relevant return depends on useful work after those costs.

A software service can often be distributed without reproducing a physical device for each deployment. Robotics introduces manufacturing, servicing and hardware replacement into the economic model. A valuation should not be mistaken for evidence that those operations have reached profitable scale.

The founder’s estimated ownership makes these company outcomes particularly relevant to a paper fortune. A large percentage can magnify exposure to changes in the business value. It does not guarantee liquidity or erase the differences between securities sold in a financing and ordinary founder holdings.

## The Archer filing is precise but historical

The reviewed [Schedule 13D amendment](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1824502/000110465922062994/tm2215981d1_sc13da.htm) identifies 32,015,625 shares beneficially owned for its stated period. It reports 15.9% and includes Class B shares convertible into Class A. This is stronger evidence than a generic insider-stock website for that date.

Beneficial ownership is a legal reporting concept. It can include shares held through entities and securities convertible under specified rules. It is not necessarily identical to a simple direct holding displayed by a tracker. Reading the footnotes matters before multiplying shares by a market price.

We have not reconciled later amendments and transactions. The 2022 count therefore stays historical. Applying today’s share price to it would assume no sales, conversions or other changes. The profile does not make that assumption, and the chart does not mix a historical stake with a current personal estimate.

## The Figure arithmetic cannot explain every asset

The wealth paragraph shows an illustrative Figure calculation with clearly identified inputs. The difference between that gross holding illustration and Forbes’s total does not prove the value of Hark or Archer. A residual can reflect other assets, different dates, discounts or provider assumptions.

The [Forbes account](https://www.forbes.com/profile/brett-adcock/) identifies Hark as a later venture and describes a financing valuation above $6 billion. It does not provide a matched founder stake for this reconstruction. We therefore do not allocate the whole company valuation to Adcock or assume a personal investment amount.

Our calculation: $23.4 billion minus $19.5 billion equals $3.9 billion. This is a difference between the attributed total and an unmatched gross Figure illustration. It is not a valuation of Hark or a verified estimate of other assets. We show the distinction to prevent a common residual-allocation error.

## Why a public filing does not solve the private-company estimate

The [historical Archer 13D amendment](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1824502/000110465922062994/tm2215981d1_sc13da.htm) supplies a share count and reporting period. It is useful for an ownership ledger. It does not authorize treating the same shares as retained in October 2026. Later transfers, sales and security changes need their own records. Historical shares should not be added to a present estimate by default.

Our analysis is that Adcock’s public and private interests require different valuation methods. A listed security has a quotation, but quantity and beneficial ownership still matter. A private holding requires a company-value assumption and security terms. An estimated Figure stake cannot settle what Archer shares remain, or whether another business contributes to the total.

The provider’s estimate is also not a realized robotics profit. Equity value reflects expectations about future results. Physical products require delivery, service and manufacturing economics before those expectations become cash flows. The reviewed records do not provide a complete operating-cost analysis for Figure. A financing valuation does not establish profitable production.

Compare [Michael Intrator’s CoreWeave profile](/leaders/michael-intrator/). There, a proxy expands the holding beyond directly registered shares. Adcock’s historical filing gives a different starting point. Both show why investigating a person requires a securities ledger, rather than a list of companies in their biography.

## What the financial record reveals

The strongest finding is that a precise public-company share count can still be unsuitable for a current wealth calculation. Its precision belongs to its date and legal definition. A newer private-company wealth estimate has a different evidence basis and should retain its attribution.

The next update should reconcile Archer’s transaction history and obtain Figure and Hark ownership evidence tied to the relevant financing dates. That would create a defensible holding-by-holding ledger. Until then, Forbes’s $23.4 billion total remains the dated personal observation.

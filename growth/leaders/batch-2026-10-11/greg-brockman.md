## The engineer before OpenAI

[Forbes’s biography](https://www.forbes.com/profile/greg-brockman/) records Brockman leaving Harvard and MIT before joining Stripe in 2010. It identifies his CTO appointment in 2013 and departure in 2015. The relevant financial lesson is the transition from helping build one private company to founding another. Neither position discloses his complete balance sheet.

His [own dated posts](https://blog.gregbrockman.com/) provide a contemporaneous account of engineering priorities. The archive includes his January 2017 discussion of the OpenAI CTO role and an earlier interest in programming languages. Unlike a retrospective net-worth list, these posts show what he was trying to build before today’s valuation debate.

A technical leadership title can describe management, architecture or direct implementation. Brockman’s writing emphasizes making other builders effective. That distinction matters when assessing a founder’s contribution. A large holding can reward organizational work accumulated over years, even when public attention concentrates on a particular product launch.

## The research organization and the capital problem

The blog archive includes a March 2019 mission post co-written with Ilya Sutskever. It discusses a changed legal structure intended to raise more capital. This is a first-person account, so we treat the rationale as the founders’ explanation rather than an independent verdict on the structure.

Our analysis is that capital formation is central to Brockman’s financial story. A research organization can need resources long before it produces revenue. Changing how investors participate can alter the relationship among mission, governance and economic claims. A reader cannot infer those claims merely from the organization’s original nonprofit description.

A founder’s estimated fortune should therefore be matched to the specific legal and economic interest being valued. A nonprofit role, a commercial shareholding and a contractual profit participation are not interchangeable. Historical structures also should not be assumed to survive later reorganizations without reviewing the applicable records.

## The $30 billion testimony and the $25.5 billion estimate

[Associated Press’s May 4, 2026 court report](https://apnews.com/article/brockman-musk-altman-openai-trial-837bdc3fbced2a02f0f93a1899260bdd) says Brockman described his OpenAI stake as worth nearly $30 billion. AP reported an $852 billion company valuation. This is reporting of testimony, rather than our inspection of the original transcript. It concerns one stake at that date, not a complete current personal balance sheet.

Our calculation: using $30 billion as an approximate comparison point, the difference from Forbes’s $25.5 billion total is $4.5 billion, or 15%. Dividing $30 billion by $852 billion gives about 3.52%. Neither calculation proves a valuation haircut or an ownership percentage. The inputs have different dates and scopes, and “nearly” supplies additional imprecision. The arithmetic measures the gap without inventing its cause.

The [December 2015 founding announcement](https://openai.com/index/introducing-openai/) named Brockman CTO and described $1 billion of donor commitments. Commitments are not cash received or spent. [OpenAI’s May 2022 announcement](https://openai.com/index/leadership-team-update/) made him president. Those records show organizational change, but do not disclose when or how his current economic interest was awarded.

The comparison is useful beside [Ilya Sutskever’s profile](/leaders/ilya-sutskever/). A disclosure about an OpenAI holding does not establish every other asset. Separate companies, security rights and provider methods must be traced separately. A reported stake and a provider’s personal total can both be meaningful without being interchangeable.
